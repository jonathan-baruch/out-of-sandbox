Day 8: Queulat Hanging Glacier to Futaleufu

Puyuhuapi still brought on the game of adventuing between our weather windows. We were very luckily to have a lot of pauses in the rainfall, but insisted that if we were going to be here, we were more than happy to hike in the rain. We had the equipment to stay dry and it was not going to be our first time. We were on our way to see the Queulat Handing Glacier, and luckily it would be another relaxing day with a very family-oriented park.

The weather at this latitude most similarly resembles Northern UK on the Northern Hemisphere, which is a unique way of traversing the world’s climates and ecosystems all in one country. Plus, the rain fall was making this feel like a real adventure.

Arriving to this glacier was such a special moment. I had been ideating on Chile and its sights for over 6 months and have been dreaming of this kind of adventure for a few years. It always felt beyond something that I would do. Then, when planning this trip, everything always felt like it was being thrown together last minute. For a while, I didn’t even think that I would be able to make Chile happen.

I’ve looked at pictures of places like the Hanging Glacier for so long. It feels surreal to have finally been standing in Chile, in the spot where all these photos were taken by others, and looking at the glacier, realizing that it really was real and doable.

The weather was still very tempermental, but seemed to be oscillating between rainfall and sunshine every 20min. Someone must have been watching over us because we decided to take the boat trek out to the glacier while it was still raining and, while getting ready to get on the boat, the weather started to clear. I figured this would only last another 20min, but it lasted our entire boat ride.

They took us down the lake towards the faces of the glacier, the granite walls, and the waterfalls coming from the ground water. The glacier spands far behind the mountains and along the tops of the range. This opening in the mountain, called the moraines, is only a face of the glacier.

Along these mountains faces, the guide told us that we can again see the lichen at play. The lichen first arrive on barren rock left behind by the glacier. It releases an acid to break down the rock and make room for moss, which then makes room to grow larger plants. These are the first stages to create the forest seen along this region.

Once back in town, we stopped for some pizza to warm up before starting our drive North.

The drive up North started to transform into, what looks like, Chile’s major agricultural region. We mostly saw cows, sheep, and goats. I assume that the region is too mountainous and the weather too variable to have too few areas that can successfully grow crops year round.

People in Chile are obsessed with the American West and cowboys, but these folks are as cowboy as they come, filled with ranches, horse riding, rodeos stadiums, their own version of a wool cowboy hat (made from wool), and Southern slang.

We stopped off at a town named La Junta to refill on gas and explore a quick 30min hike to the local caves and viewpoint on the city.

When we finally arrived at the road split, I said the final good byes to Michal and Karina, and dropped them off in Villa Santa Lucia at the edge of Yelcho lake where Ruta 7 splits towards Chaiten and another off-road route would take me to Futaleufu.

We were both the longer hitching experience any of us had had, and I was so happy to have made such good friends along the way. Poland is an amazing country filled with history, culture, food, and backpacking. I hope to go visit some day soon.

The road to Futaleufu goes through a mountain pass, deep within its valleys, where its river is home to water sport adventures. I was most excited to take a break from the driving and backpacks and leave them behind for some guided excusions on the river.
