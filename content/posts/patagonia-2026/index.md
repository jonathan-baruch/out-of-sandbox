After yesterday's travels, I decided to sleep in some more today and enjoy a bit of this quiant Hostel made up of old typewriters, a fireplace, and sliding doors counter-weighted by knitted hens. I hit the road by 8AM to get a headstart on the day since I would have the long drive ahead of me. I grabbed gas at the local Copec Station and began my way down South for Puerto Rio Tranquillo, but had no idea what magic the day had in store for me.

This was my first big driving day and was initially planned for 4hrs to do a shorter hike to a glacier. Chile, on the other hand, seems have a funny way of making you slow down a little.

The road that leads down South was carved out by the glaciers still seen in the Aysén Region. From the mountains, we can see the oldest visible layer from marine sediments, the lighter layer from volcanic ash, and the red/brown tones from iron oxidation. 

{{< resping src="images/Day1/mountain_layers.png" size="1000x" caption="Hitory sandwiched in time" >}}

The colors of the rivers are from "glacial flour" that has ground the bedrock into a fine powder, which, when suspended in the water and reflected off the sun, turns the rivers into a milky-turquiose glow.


{{< resping src="images/Day1/river_colors.png" size="1000x" >}}
{{< resping src="images/Day1/river_bend_to_mountains.png" size="1000x" >}}

After about an hours drive, I reached the end of the paved Carretera Austral. This year marks the 50th anniversary of the Carretera Austral, but construction is long and dangerous along these roads due to landslides and materials.

During the original construction, this land used to be consider impassable taking crews a month to traverse 20 meters in some areas. And now, all you need is a 4x4. Still, work continues every day to open this route to more travelers.

{{< resping src="images/Day1/fin_pavimento.png" size="1000x" caption="Pavement Ends" >}}

The road from here took much longer than I initially planned. Dust would be kicked up everywhere - they would only wet the ground near construction zones for the 5 man crews that spread along the road. It took some time to get comfortable on loose gravel. Service would break in and out for a hour at a time. So, I took my time to ease into the roads winding along the valley floors.

{{< resping src="images/Day1/dusty_road.png" size="1000x" caption="" >}}

Chile is so big that it stretches from desserts to highlands to forests to glaciers to wetlands to towns. It was refreshingly surprising to come across a, somewhat, rain forest where the air was 10 degrees cooler and leaves larger than my torso!

{{< resping src="images/Day1/forest_road.png" size="1000x" caption="A muddy rainforest that smelled of fresh dew and mud breaking up the dusty air" >}}

{{< resping src="images/Day1/dusty_trees.png" size="1000x" caption="And now a dusty forest" >}}

When the air started to clear and I could see a bend in the road, I needed to stretch my legs. Not only did this place have service to check in with those back home, but I was sandwiched by a turquise river and a sea of wildflowers that stretched to the largest glacier that I had yet seen in Chile. I knew that this would be a perfect place for a coffee break. I setup by stove, fired up the water and fresh beans from Unimarc, and enjoyed the sunshine on my face, sipping on an Americano, chatting with my mom and girlfriend.

I remember before my trip telling my dad that Chile was just feeling like an idea and didn't feel real or tangible. He advised that I just need to get my two feet on the ground and it'll start to come together.

This coffee break, that view, and the feeling of both being far from reality and so close to it at the same time, started to get my two feet back down to earth.

As I kept driving, the Rio Tranquillo started to open up before me and I couldn't stop myself from slowing down traffic to take it all in. In a few proper pull outs, I couldn't help but make a few friends along the way.

Gene was born in Riverside and lived in Indiana after graduating from university there. Today, she lives in Santa Cruz, CA as a data analyst for a conservation non-profit. The world really is small! She is travelling with a Chilean friend that also lives in Indiana and hasn't been back to Southern Chile is 42 years. When she was last here, this road didn't exist and the only way to get here was by boat.

A quick mention about these dogs: I've noticed that in Balmaceda, Coyhaique, along the roads, and later in Rio Tranquillo dogs seem to be everywhere. No collars, just free-roaming. They stand outside of stores waiting for food, sit along street corners, and play in the same. They all seem to be well-fed, but clearly wild. Still, people seem to show them a lot of affection and keep them healthy.

As I neared Puerto Rio Tranquillo, I could feel the excitement starting. This place was unbelivably beautiful. I no longer cared about doing the planned hike today. Sometimes the plan needs to be thrown out and you just need to see what the day has planned to offer you.

I arrived in town around 2PM with a whole afternoon to spare. The town of Puerto Rio Tranquillo, or Puerto Tranquillo, was an old settler town of ranchers from Chile and Argentina that only ended its isolation in the early 1990s when the Carretera Austral finally reached the town. It is so small that the length is only 4 x 2 blocks. In talking with locals, they seems to come here in the summer time when tourism to the famous Marble Caves and other hiking treks are at its peak, then head back to cities like Santiago. Still, the city is surprisingly developed with flourishing businesses centered around high-adventure, cameras and modern lighting the streets for safety, and a park that I can only wish I grew up around with a special focus on child education.

{{< resping src="images/Day1/boat_dock.png" size="1000x" caption="Boats that take visitors to the Marble Caves" >}}

So, like the pioneers before me, this was a perfect place to find my footing.

I took a swim in the water. I just couldn't help myself. Even though it was from the glaciers, I would say that it was warmer than the water back at home. It just felt warm enough outside, that I needed to jump in. Though I should mentione that there were others with coats, but I was definitely overheating. 
The water out here is some of the cleanest in the world. It was like floating on air to be swimming in water that felt so smooth and was tasteless.

Once I changed back to my clothes, I needed a little walk to warm back up. I walked the town and explored the shops. I checked in with my kayaking guide that I would use tomorrow, Gerald from ChileAndes. He recommended a few markets and a dinner spot with a lamb burger that I would try later that event, which had a kind of punk rock feel, made their own craft beer with free samples, and played RedBull documentaries on the TVs. 

Though that would come later, I couldn't help myself but pick up a Cervesa Austral and a freshly made slice of rasberrry cheesecake. What I love about this place, and makes it the most difficult, is that most folks don't speak any English, so I faked my way through and seemed to have managed alright. I brought those back to the shore and decided that this was going to be my day.

Here's something that I wrote earlier today while sitting on that shoreline:

There's nothing like the feeling of the last weeks of summer. The hot air starts to cool but hasn't yet become Autumn so hits a perfect temperature. The sun lies like a gentle, warm blanket over the air that matches your body temperature and seems to lose all feeling. You're sitting on a log in one of the world's most remote and beautiful places. Boats are taking tourists to marble caves, the mountains are lined with trees, flowers, granite, and glaciers at the same time. You think that it's supposed to be cold given the heavy clouds over the mountains and for how south you are, but the winds carry in a warmth. No cities or paved roads for miles, hardly any English, a long way from home, a fresh rasberry cheescake in hand, and time moves as slowly as the mountains, and rushes past as fast as the rivers. Two dogs wrestle on the beach and chase each other up and down. Two men my age share a coffee on the next log, but their Spanish and the music playing from the nearby restaurant is washed away by the sound of the waves. There's no where to be. Every store is within a couple blocks reach, and not a thought or worry in mind.

I think I found my favorite zen. I think I found my footing in Chile.
