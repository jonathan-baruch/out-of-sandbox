I woke up this morning in a river that overlooked the Rio Baker with the mountains in the backdrop. Not a bad way to start off the day. With some boxed wine and a full dinner last night, I was ready to start my day.

It was a slow morning of having breakfast, playing with the cats and dogs as we all sat out getting our sunshine and starting to warm up. The cats were either scrating up against the tree bark, the kitten wanting to play fight, and all of them alternating between the sun and some shade under by car.

I kicked off the day by taking advantage of the location. People travel from all around to see La Confluencia, where the Rio Baker joins the Rio Neff. From this house, it is only a 15min walk along the river and passed some (very exclusive) "private property" signs. For $33 for the night, it was the cheapest luxury I've ever had.

Watching LA Confluencia is completely mesmorizing. Though both rivers originate from glaciers, the Rio Neff appears brown/grey because it comes directly from the glacier, carrying high concentration of fresh rock powders. Meanwhile, the Rio Baker stays turquiose because its sediments have had time to settle in large lakes like Lake Bertrand and General Carrera (where I kayaked yesterday) before reaching the confluence. As the rivers meet, there are some distinguishing lines, but in other areas the two rivers push back and forth intermixing between both colors.

After I was able to enjoy the time by the river, it was time for me to head out towards Pataginia National Park. What I didn't expect along the way was that, this far South, the roads are still be excavated for wider lanes and, eventually, paved roads. Seeing the amount of work that goes into using dynamite and drills to break up rock, move it, clear road space, keep the ground moist to prevent too much dust, all while allowing cars to pass along a 1.5 wide car route, really makes you appreciate what it takes for a country to invest in its infrastrucutre and build & maintain millions of miles of paved roads.

Driving along these roads, you need to ride close to the shoulder when passing incoming cars driving at nearly 50-60 mi/hr, or wait sometimes 10min while workers and drivers clear the road to allow another batch of cars to pass through. This all made getting to Patagonia National Park take an extra hour.

Entering Patagonia National Park, I first passed through the Valle de Chacabuco and was pleasantly welcomed by Gunancos - the ancestor of the llama. Guanacos help to spread vegetation across the region, are the natural preditor for pumas in the area, and held key survival and spiritual importance to the natives back in the day. But, in the end, who can deny that adorable face?

The hike in the park that I chose to do was the Lagunas Atlas trail that would take me on a 20km loop up the mountain ridge and traverse along a series of mountain lakes. I'll let the pictures do most of the talking today, but I will say that it was a hike to remember.

For one, the hike was estimated to be 6-8hrs. With a race against the end of the day, I managed to finish in 4.5hrs, including water, food, picture, and a phone call break (Starlink came in handy to Facetime my mom and girlfriend atop the mountain to share the journey). My legs were feeling good, the sun was keeping the day warm, and I just felt like I was flying.

Second, it was my first time to have an entire mountain nearly all to myself. For hours, I walked and all I could hear was my breathing, the wind, occasionally a bird, and a few guanacos at the end of the hike. There's something so profound to be standing atop a mountain in the Southern-most parts of the world, alone. It wasn't in a unsafe way (I didn't make sure to bring plenty of emergency back-ups), but in a connected way, a feeling that was surprisingly like I wasn't too far from home yet allowed to experience the nature for everything it had to give and, in return, I could give my attention and my energy back.

Third, it was the first time that I could experience the Autumn colors out here. On previous days, the grasses had either started to dry out after the summer or the climate was so humid that the trees were always green. Out there, you could really see that we are on the cusp of a new season. Right as the weather is still warm and starting to cool, and the leaves were starting to turn yellow and red, constrasting the rocks around them.
