I woke up at 6:30am this morning to catch my reservation to kayak to Rio Tranquillo's most notable highlight - Marble Caves. It started off a bit misty, but quickly cleared off as the sun rose for sunrise. Luckily it had rained this morning, since it means that the winds don't pick up over the water leaving us with a glassy lake.

It turns out that the color of the lake is not only made up of the "glacier powder" that I understood yesterday, but is also caused by the light reflecting off of the marble that lines the lake floor up into the mountains. The white of the marble is made up of calcium carbonate. The calcium originated from the marine like that once filled the Aysen Region. Once the tectonic plates split, this region rose up to expose its ocean floors, creating the vast mountain ranges seen today.

The white "moss" on the rocks is lichen, which is known to be some of the first life outside of the water. They live in extreme environments and act as a vital bioindicator of clean air.

What's funny about these caves is that the locals never considered them to be special. Only until the 2000s was Puerto Rio Tranquillo a town of herders and agriculture. Once Chileans started to visit along the Carretera Austral and want to visit these rocks out in the water, did the town's economy re-focus to tourism to take travelers to and from the caves by boat or kayak.

Once back in town, I stopped by a bakery for an empanada and a latte before planning to head out of town. While there, I met two Chileans guys, Luis (aka Lucho) and Antonio. Antonio is a sound engineer who works performances for some of the biggest artists in Chile. Lucho studied tourism in university and used to work as a park ranger at Parque Exploradores, a ecological park 50km from town along a road that I had been recommended two times in the last two days.

The guys had just picked up a cake and had been trying to get to the park for the past two days to visit another fellow park ranger who would drive the three of them to a river festival in a town (just a fjord over), where they would be providing free rafting trips for the community.

I was not about to miss this opportunity to explore this park with a professional park ranger.

{{< resping src="images/Day2/cake_time.png" size="1000x" caption="Antonio, Lucho, and me stealing some slices of cake in front of a river view on its way to be delivered" >}}

Along the drive, I learned a few fun clarifications. 

First, the reason that we see so many pets walking the streets is because, in this southern rural park of Chile, animals are not seen as house pets. Instead, they are seen for their functional use. The cow is for milk, chickens for eggs, horses to ride, dogs to herd the sheep, and cats to catch the mice. People own their own animals and will let them wander. Part of the reason not to bring them inside or show too much affection is because they aren't seen as being very clean and so that they don't get overly domenticated and stop being effective.

Second, there is a native fruit from Patagonia called Calafate Berry that they use to make Cerveza Austral. It is said that those who eat or drink this berry are destined to return to Patagonia. This is probably why I felt so good today.

I've been told that Chile would be slow moving and I thought this meant that Chileans would be slow to get things done. This is unlike any other road trip in that time just moves differently. The roads are longer, rivers rush by quickly, mountains move imperceptibly, and things are scaled from the very large to the very very small.

Once we arrived at Parque Exploradores, we were pleasantly greeted by the staff (happy that we had brought cake from town). The staff works up here 12 days with 3 days off throughout the season. Lucho had maintained most of the trails found in the park, and was amazing at pointing out every details we could think to ask. This was Antonio's first time here as well, so it was nice to share the experience with someone else.

Once we were able to put our bags down, we headed out to explore the forests and glacier views. Chile is really made up of the small and the big details. The forests in this area are green year round. Due to all the moisture, fires are the least of their concern. The roots of the trees are so shallow that they stretch across the floor every which way. Since it is so wet, stepping on them will cause you to slip even with the newest rubber boots. Antonio calls slipping on these roots a "plop" - trust me, I know from my own (2x) personal experience.

With such a shallow root system, it really speaks to the interconnectivity of this forest plants.

Seeing the glaciers was a highlight of this trip that I was very excited to see. I couldn't be happier to be out here, not a cell tower or town in 50km, and only the sound of the wind and waterfalls coming down the mountain to listen to on such a warm day.

*A moment about conservation*

Chile has over 24,000 glaciers with only one that is growing in size. It is estmated that by about the year 2200, all of the world's glaciers are expected to disappear. Something that I also experienced in Iceland. This glacier was known to be stable for many years, only recently has it started to change day-to-day receding by 6 meters per year.

Chile is also second to Antartica as the place for scientists to study its water. The PH of the ocean water here is similar to what scientists predict for the global oceans in the year 2100, so the marine life and coral reefs are studied as predictors for the futrue.

What makes today most unique is that it is the inauguration day for Chile's new president. Locals here say that he does not believe in human impacts on gloabl warming and plans to extract the natural resources in ways that the local feel threatened.

As I said my good-byes, I turned on my own Starlink for the first time on this trip. The setup was surprisingly easy and took only a few minutes to connect to the car, find a signal, and let me family know that I was safe and on my way back to town. With the rains misting all day, there had been a rainbow that held over Rio Tranquillo since the kayakking trip that, even without a Starlink, I had my North Star back.

In town, I filled up with the last gas that I would have for the next few days, and headed to my southern-most part of this trek - Parque National Patagonia.

The drive down was just along golden hour and I couldn't help but feel at peace with how the day unfolded. Being this late in the season, I've had rooms to myself this whole trip and tonight I would have a whole house on my own overlooking the Confluencia, where the Rio Baker and Rio Neff meet.

Tonight I writing next to an old wood-fire oven listening to the rain fall outside with some boxed wine in hand. I can't wait for what tomorrow has in store.
